Media Briefing · EEA-ESEM 2026

One banking panic, two Fed responses—and local economic differences still visible a decade later

In 1930, a major banking panic hit Mississippi at a time when the state was divided between two Federal Reserve districts that reacted in very different ways. The Atlanta Fed expanded credit to the banking system, while the St. Louis Fed contracted it. I find that banks exposed to the Atlanta response were much less likely to fail permanently and, importantly, that differences between the two sides were still visible in local labor markets ten years later. At the same time, the effects on the people originally living there were much smaller, in part because people on the more negatively affected St. Louis side were more likely to move.

Paper: Places and People Under Heterogeneous Federal Reserve Policy

Author: Humberto Martínez-García, El Colegio de México

Conference: EEA-ESEM 2026, Dublin

Why this matters

When banks start failing, one of the main questions for a central bank is how aggressively it should intervene. This question is particularly difficult to answer empirically because monetary policy normally applies to an entire country at the same time. We therefore rarely observe similar places facing the same shock but receiving different monetary-policy responses.

Mississippi in 1930 provides an unusual exception.

Following the collapse of Caldwell & Company, one of the largest financial conglomerates in the South, bank failures increased dramatically across the region. At that time Mississippi was divided between the Atlanta and St. Louis Federal Reserve districts, and the two districts had very different views about how to respond to a banking panic.

The Atlanta Fed took a more interventionist approach and expanded credit to the banking system. The St. Louis Fed did the opposite and contracted its credit supply.

This creates a rare natural experiment. Places that were geographically very close, and that looked similar before the crisis, were exposed to sharply different Federal Reserve policies simply because they were located on different sides of the district boundary.

Key findings

  • The more expansionary Atlanta response substantially reduced permanent bank failures. Among banks close to the Federal Reserve district boundary, banks on the Atlanta side were about 14 percentage points less likely to fail permanently during the Caldwell-related banking crisis.
  • The effects did not end with the banking panic. Ten years later, I still find important differences in local labor markets. Residents on the Atlanta side were about 2 percentage points less likely to be unemployed using the Census definition of unemployment, which included public emergency work. They were also less likely to rely on public emergency jobs and worked fewer weeks during the previous year.
  • The results look very different when we follow people instead of places. When I follow the individuals who were originally living close to the Federal Reserve boundary in 1930, most of the differences found for local labor markets become much smaller or disappear. At the same time, people originally on the St. Louis side were 5 percentage points more likely to leave their county and 2 percentage points more likely to leave Mississippi by 1940.

Taken together, these results suggest that migration was an important way in which people responded to the more negative shock on the St. Louis side. The effects remained visible in the places that received the contractionary policy, but people had the possibility of moving somewhere else.

What the study does

The paper combines historical bank-level information that I collected from the Rand McNally Bankers' Directories with individual-level information from the complete 1930 and 1940 U.S. Censuses.

The key feature of the research design is the boundary between the Atlanta and St. Louis Federal Reserve districts in Mississippi. I focus on a relatively narrow area around this boundary and show that, before the banking panic, the two sides looked very similar in terms of banking conditions, labor-market outcomes, and other economic characteristics.

I use a data-driven procedure to determine how far from the boundary this comparison remains credible. The resulting area extends approximately 67 kilometres, or 42 miles, on each side of the boundary.

Within this area, the main relevant difference after the Caldwell collapse was the Federal Reserve policy to which each side was exposed.

The historical bank data allow me to study the immediate effect on bank failures. I then use the full-count Census to study what happened to local labor markets ten years later. Finally, by linking individuals between the 1930 and 1940 Censuses, I can follow people who originally lived near the boundary even if they subsequently moved somewhere else in the United States.

This last step is what allows me to distinguish between what happened to places and what happened to people.

Places versus people

This distinction is one of the main points of the paper.

When I study places, I ask what happened to people living in the local labor markets on the two sides of the boundary in 1940. Here I find substantial differences. Residents on the Atlanta side were less likely to be unemployed, less likely to be in public emergency work, and worked fewer weeks during the previous year while earning similar annual wage income.

But this does not necessarily tell us what happened to the people who actually experienced the original shock in 1930. Some of them may have left.

I therefore also study people by following individuals who lived near the boundary in 1930 to wherever they were living ten years later. Once I do this, most of the differences become considerably smaller.

Importantly, migration itself was different across the two sides. People originally on the St. Louis side were more likely to leave both their county and the state.

Thus, the results point to a simple way of reconciling the place and people effects. Local economies cannot move when they are hit by a negative shock. People can.

What the findings mean today

The Federal Reserve of 1930 was very different from the Federal Reserve today, and the magnitude of these historical estimates should not be directly applied to current monetary policy.

But the episode helps answer a broader question that is still relevant: what can happen when a central bank responds aggressively to banking distress, compared with allowing credit conditions to contract during a panic?

The first result is perhaps the most immediate one: the more expansionary Atlanta policy was associated with substantially fewer permanent bank failures.

But the main message of the paper goes beyond banks. I find that the different monetary responses were associated with differences in local labor-market outcomes that were still present ten years later.

At the same time, the comparison between places and people shows that these long-run local effects do not necessarily translate one-for-one into equally persistent effects on the individuals initially exposed to them. People can respond, and one important response in this episode was migration.

This distinction matters when evaluating the consequences of large economic shocks. Looking only at the people who experienced the shock may understate what happened to the places they left behind.

Important qualifications

This is a historical natural experiment based on Mississippi during the Great Depression. The results come from communities relatively close to the Atlanta–St. Louis Federal Reserve boundary, and the Federal Reserve operated very differently at the time. The numerical estimates should therefore not be mechanically extrapolated to modern monetary policy.

The sample that allows me to follow the same people from 1930 to 1940 is also more selected than the sample used to study local labor markets and, because of the limitations of historical record linkage, consists almost entirely of men.

Finally, the migration results are consistent with migration being an important channel through which people mitigated the negative shock they faced. The paper does not, however, separately estimate exactly how much of the difference between the place and people effects is caused by migration.

Author quote

“What makes this episode useful is that we have places that were very close to each other and were hit by the same banking panic, but received very different Federal Reserve responses. I find that the more active response reduced permanent bank failures and that differences in local labor markets were still present ten years later. But when I follow the people who originally lived there, most of those differences become much smaller. Migration appears to be an important part of the reason why.”

— Humberto Martínez-García, El Colegio de México
